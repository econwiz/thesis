doremus notes

Doremus aims to understand the difference in energy spending between households of different poverty levels. They find that low-income HH spending is much less responsive to extreme weather than high-income spending, both for electricity and natural gas (in the winter). This gap in consumption is referred to as the "poverty gap". They find that these differences do not have to do with low-income HHs needed less energy, and that the results hold for different house sizes. The results are also not driven by changes in AC availability: it is not a question of if the HH has AC but whether they can afford to run it. 

"Our estimates corroborate surveys and qualitative studies that find energy insecurity is widespread among low-income households, and imply policies that raise energy prices will disproportionately impact low-income households. The symmetry of our findings – poverty gaps in energy spending that are of similar magnitudes for both hot and cold weather – suggests energy assistance programs focused primarily on winter heating costs may miss a substantial part of the burden of energy bills."

This is the application to LIHEAP, since it mainly accounts for winter bills and not summertime ones. It also implies that systematic differences in energy prices that disadvantage low-income HHs would exacerbate this effect (i.e. utility structure)

"We engage with these themes more formally by developing a theoretical model of household energy consumption (see Appendix A). The model incorporates household preferences over reducing health risks from exposure to extreme weather, emphasizing the distinction between willingness- and ability-to-pay for energy spending. Extensions include weather-dependent household income, energy prices that increase with income, and income-associated differences in energy needs." 

Poverty gap is exacerbated in the winter and summer months. 
Their specification does not prove causality of income on expenditure. Causal identification of that would require exogenous changes in temperature and poverty year over year. 

What I am doing that is different: First, I am testing a mechanism behind this (on top of budgetary constraints), which is the type of utility that serves a low-income HH might additionally impact their gap relative to high-income HHs. This is important as it signals a need for LIHEAP reevaluation as well as uniform regulation amongst utilities (to a reasonable degree). I am also studying effects at the utility-year level, rather than the HH level. Therefore my effects are not necessarily generalizable to a given HH's consumption patterns, but with the appropriate use of weights could be generalized to income-territory groups writ large.
