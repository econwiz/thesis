Abstract: This paper examines the effect of ownership structure on the distribution of household electricity costs and its implications for income inequality. We leverage data on household electricity expenditure, income, and utility tariff structures to provide new insights into the comparative merits of alternative ownership regimes in the U.S. electricity sector. We use ownership discontinuities between adjacent statistical areas to establish causal effects. We find strong evidence that electricity costs are more regressive under cooperative and public ownership, resulting in undesirable distributional outcomes. Households served by cooperative and publicly-owned utilities spend a larger share of their income on electricity than those served by private utilities. We present suggestive evidence that high fixed charges and limited segmentation of economically diverse consumer groups are potential mechanisms driving the observed regressivity of electricity costs. Our findings highlight the role of firms' ownership structures and pricing strategies in shaping existing income inequality by determining how energy burdens are distributed across income groups.

Ownership bundles regulatory exposure with internal governance, and a cross-section of ownership types cannot separate them. I break the bundle three ways, each holding ownership fixed while one component varies. First, the staggered imposition and lifting of state disconnection moratoria between 2020 and 2022 varies consumer protection within utility over time; if disconnection risk drives the gap, both the cooperative and municipal differentials should compress toward the investor-owned baseline while orders bind, and a movement in only one rules the regulatory channel out. Second, comparing cooperatives to municipal utilities in states where neither is rate-regulated holds regulatory status fixed and isolates the difference between member-board and council governance — the contrast that generates the sign reversal in my preliminary results, which Peltzman's fiscal-political mechanism predicts for municipals and cannot produce for cooperatives. Third, within cooperatives, the non-residential share of sales proxies the weight of large commercial and agricultural members in board elections, providing continuous variation in governance intensity with regulation, rurality and wholesale structure all held constant.
